I oppose the motion.

Strict laws to regulate LLMs would do more harm than good because they would freeze innovation, entrench big tech, and punish the very people most likely to benefit from this technology.

First, overregulation creates a barrier to entry. Large companies can afford lawyers, compliance teams, audits, and licensing requirements; startups, researchers, and open-source developers usually cannot. So “strict” laws don’t just regulate LLMs — they hand the market to a few giants and reduce competition. That is the opposite of accountability.

Second, LLMs are a general-purpose tool, like the internet, search engines, or word processors. Regulating the tool too harshly because it can be misused is a blunt response. We already have laws against fraud, defamation, harassment, discrimination, and data theft. The right approach is to enforce those existing laws against harmful use, not create heavy new restrictions on the technology itself.

Third, strict regulation will slow beneficial uses in medicine, education, accessibility, and productivity. LLMs can help students learn, assist disabled users, support doctors with paperwork, and help small businesses compete. If we burden development with rigid rules and approval processes, we delay these benefits and lock in the status quo.

Finally, “strict laws” sound reassuring, but in practice they are often too slow and too rigid to keep up with fast-moving technology. By the time lawmakers catch up, the rules are outdated or misapplied. A better solution is targeted, flexible oversight: transparency where needed, penalties for real harm, and strong enforcement of existing laws.

So the issue is not whether LLMs should be governed — they should. The issue is whether we should impose strict laws that stifle progress and concentrate power. We should not.
